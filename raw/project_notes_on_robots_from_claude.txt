Architected solenoid-based actuator strategy over servo alternatives
Architected solenoid-based actuator strategy over servo alternatives
Using the arms as a v0 is totally reasonable for positioning and for the start button — I wouldn't throw them out. The move is to stop asking the arm to be the thing that's fast. Here's the principle that reframes the whole actuation design:

Decouple positioning from actuation. The SO-ARM101's job becomes "get a fast actuator onto the flipper button and hold it there," which is slow, one-time-per-machine, and well within what a 5-DOF hobby-servo arm and GR00T can do. The reflex-speed press comes from a dedicated actuator mounted as the arm's end-effector. The arm never has to move quickly — it parks the effector on the button and holds; the effector fires in milliseconds. That single split resolves the latency problem without giving up the arm's adaptability across different cabinets.

For the actuator itself, here's the menu from lowest latency up, with the tradeoffs that matter for your showroom context:

The theoretical floor is electrical — tapping a solid-state relay or optocoupler across the flipper button's leaf switch, so the robot "presses" the button electronically in well under a millisecond. Builders of pinball-playing bots have done exactly this. But it means opening the coin door and splicing into the cabinet wiring, and these are Stern's showroom demo units, not yours — so I'd treat electrical bypass as off-limits and assume you need a non-invasive physical press. Worth knowing it exists as the latency benchmark you're measuring against.

The practical sweet spot is a small push-solenoid as the fingertip. Mount a short-stroke solenoid (you only need to cover the button's few millimeters of travel and overcome its spring — a few newtons) on each arm's end-effector. Coil energize to full stroke is roughly 5-15ms depending on the solenoid, and the MOSFET driving it switches in sub-millisecond time, so your actuation term lands in the single-digit-to-low-teens of milliseconds — the range reflex flipping actually needs, and roughly an order of magnitude better than commanding an STS3215 to jab. Drive each with a logic-level MOSFET, flyback diode across the coil, gate off a microcontroller. Two important refinements: run the solenoids on a separate power rail from Thor (their inrush spikes have no business on your compute supply — convenient since you already have a 24V-ish battery domain), and use peak-and-hold current control for cradling. Holding a flipper up to trap the ball means keeping the solenoid energized, which cooks it at full current; the fix is high current for pull-in then reduced hold current — which is, satisfyingly, exactly the dual-wind/PWM trick real pinball machines use on their own flipper coils.

A fast digital servo finger (say ~0.05s/60°) is the middle option — simpler wiring, no separate rail drama, but ~20-40ms and it'll wear under thousands of rapid presses. Fine for the start button, marginal for reflex flips. I'd use a servo (or the arm directly) for start/plunger and solenoids for the flippers.

Two mechanical things to get right early. First, registration: the SO-101 isn't stiff, and it'll be holding position through cabinet vibration and its own servo jitter for a whole game. Give the effector a compliant tip with a shallow cup or guide that seats over the button housing so it physically can't walk off the button — let the mechanical seat do the precision, not the arm's holding accuracy. Second, reach geometry: flipper buttons sit on both sides of the cabinet near the front, ~55cm apart on a standard body (wider on a widebody), and your arms are ~40cm reach. With the base centered and close in front, each arm reaching its own side is plausible but tight and depends on your base width and how far forward the arms mount — I'd mock this up with cardboard against an actual cabinet before committing the effector design, because if the geometry doesn't close you may want the solenoids on short dedicated brackets the arms place and clamp rather than continuously hold.

And keep the solenoid delay in context: it's just one term in your reflex budget. The others are camera exposure/readout, ball-tracker inference, the decision, and the trigger comms. Use a high-FPS global-shutter camera on the playfield (rolling shutter will smear a fast ball), and keep the trigger path short — if the tracker runs on Thor, fire the flip over a direct GPIO/UART line to the solenoid MCU rather than routing it through a slow USB stack or ROS topic. Once you characterize the solenoid's mechanical delay (scope it, or high-speed-video it), feed that constant forward into your flip timing so the decision fires early by exactly that offset.

Do you know yet what playfield camera and frame rate you're planning? That, plus where the ball tracker runs, sets the rest of your reflex budget — and it determines whether the solenoid's ~10ms is comfortably inside your window or the thing you end up fighting.
